Personal Essay

The screen of my aging phone goes black and dies, taking the half-written Python script with it. I yelled, not out of frustration, but from familiarity. This phone, my only computer, is becoming an unreliable partner in my efforts to understand the digital world. I first entered this world because I needed textbooks, not for social media nor games.

By the time I turned four, my parents had separated, mainly because of my father's addiction and the harm it brought to our family. My mother took my younger brother and me to our grandparents' home, where our elder brother lived. She opened a small shop, but it was not profitable. After that, she began selling shoes at the market, even though she became sick with kidney disease, just to keep us alive.

When I was old enough to go to school, I registered in 2014 at Abomsa Primary School Number 1. In Grade 3, if my uncle hadn't bought me an exercise book, I would have stopped going to school. I remember that my grades were bad until Grade 3. In Grade 1, I ranked 33rd out of 60 students. By Grade 4, watching my mother struggle to live without financial stability in my early childhood helped me realize that studying would change both my grades and my future life. I also hoped to help others who faced hardship like mine. Since then, my grades improved from D's to A's and B's.

In Grade 8, the Oromia Development Association started a project in our area. It gathered the best-performing students from across Merti Woreda and taught them with the best teachers, but only if they passed an entrance exam. I got the chance to take the exam and scored the highest on the entrance exam. However, the Ministry of Education had changed the old curriculum to new ones, and its textbooks were only available online. This marked the beginning of another challenge: my mother could not afford a mobile phone for me to learn with.

We had half-day classes in the morning. After school, I ate lunch and went to the library to search for the concepts related to our new curriculum. After only three hours of sleep, I woke up in the middle of the night to read straight through until the morning. Ranking third in that elite class was not a defeat; it was a great victory for me to achieve without access to the textbooks. Seeing this, my aunt gave me my very first mobile phone. Holding that phone opened a new world for me: the digital world. Since it was my first time exploring it, I became curious about how digital systems functioned. I began learning Python and cybersecurity on my phone. However, the lack of a computer made practice more challenging, but through it all I kept using my phone to code and learn.

Although my phone shuts down while I am coding, I refuse to give up. Seeing some of my friends get hacked, I conducted research on the current cyber threats in Ethiopia. As a result, I built some cybersecurity-related tools. For instance, I built a basic phishing link detection tool in Python to create awareness and to teach my peers. In my small town, where digital awareness and infrastructure are limited, I teach my peers what I learn. This realization of vulnerabilities in systems extends beyond the screen.

I still code on the phone that once shut down while I was coding, and I still teach my peers despite lacking a computer. It has been a long time since I stopped waiting for the perfect tools. I am building with what I have, from the deepest interest once emerged from seeing the vulnerable systems—for the family that sacrificed everything, for the town that taught me resilience.
